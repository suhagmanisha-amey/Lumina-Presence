## 1. Gartner's "by 2026" middle-management prediction has hit its deadline year

**What it is:** Gartner predicted that *by 2026*, 20% of organisations would use AI to eliminate more than half of current middle-management roles. We are now in Q4 of that year, and the operational data is tracking with it: Q1 2026 saw 78,000+ global tech layoffs with nearly half attributed to AI automation, and the specific thing being cut is the *coordination* function — status reporting, tracking, translating up and down — that defined mid-tier roles. India-specific: 12,000+ cuts across firms including TCS, hitting mid-to-senior talent hardest; TCS headcount fell to 582,163 by end-December from 607,979 the prior March.

**Why it matters to this audience specifically:** Lumina's audience is exactly the demographic in the crosshairs — qualified managers whose value has historically been demonstrated through *throughput* (I coordinated this, I tracked that, I ran the deck). That proof-of-value is the part being automated. What survives automation is judgment, bearing, and the ability to hold a room — which is literally the product. This is the single clearest argument for why presence stopped being a "nice-to-have" and became risk mitigation.

**Why this week:** The prediction's named year is three months from expiry, and Indian IT reports quarterly headcount in early-month results disclosures (as the TCS December figure shows) — so Q2 FY27 numbers land in this window. The conversation is live, not hypothetical.

## 2. Gallup's new India report: the risk is people who show up but aren't invested

**What it is:** Gallup published *Rebuilding Indian Workplaces for the Future* roughly three weeks ago. Its framing is notable — it explicitly argues India is *not* facing a surge of actively hostile employees; the real risk is the growing population of people physically present at work but not psychologically invested in the role.

**Why it matters to this audience:** This is the clinical description of what happens to someone who's been overlooked long enough — the person whose idea gets ignored until a colleague repeats it eventually stops offering ideas. Lumina's audience usually reads that as a personal failing ("I've lost my edge"). A Gallup report reframes it as a predictable, structural outcome. That reframe is persuasive without being self-pitying, and it opens the door to "here's the behavioural fix" rather than "here's why your company is bad."

**Why this week:** It's the freshest substantial India-specific workplace dataset in the window — published ~mid-September 2026, so it's still in the period where commentary gets traction rather than being yesterday's news.

## 3. The bell curve is being deliberately sharpened — fewer people can be "excellent"

**What it is:** Deloitte's India Talent Outlook puts the 2026 average increment at 9.1% (vs 9.0% in 2025) — flat. But underneath the flat number, organisations are sharpening the bell curve: the share of employees receiving the top rating on a five-point scale fell 300 basis points, from 10% in 2024 to 7% in 2025. Aon's 32nd Annual Salary Increase survey lands at the same 9.1% headline. IT services specifically is running 7–8% this cycle — the leanest since 2020 — while people who switch jobs command 20–35%.

**Why it matters to this audience:** This is the mechanism behind "I'm qualified but overlooked," stated in numbers. When only 7 in 100 can be rated top, the deciding factor stops being output quality and becomes *who the decision-makers can picture in the senior role*. That is a presence problem, not a performance problem — and it's the most defensible version of Lumina's core claim. It also explains why strong performers are getting average ratings and concluding something is wrong with them.

**Why this week:** Indian organisations are in the Q3/H2 window where mid-year calibration and ratings forecasting happen ahead of the next cycle — the decisions that produce April's rating are being shaped now, not in April.

## 4. foundit: half of India's professionals are already job-hunting, and managers aren't helping

**What it is:** foundit's Appraisal Survey found appraisals running late this year and more than half of surveyed professionals already job-hunting. A standout finding: a large share said their manager did little for them through the appraisal process.

**Why it matters to this audience:** Two distinct audiences in one data point. For the overlooked individual contributor or mid-level manager, it validates that the fix often isn't internal — and if they're interviewing, every one of those conversations is a high-stakes room where bearing and tone decide the offer band (see the 20–35% switcher premium above). For the senior leaders in Lumina's audience, it's a mirror: *they* are the manager who did little, and losing half a team is a command failure, not an HR problem.

**Why this week:** Delayed cycles push the attrition decision into Q3/Q4, which collides with the pre-festive-season window when Indian professionals traditionally time resignations and moves. The job-search conversation is at its loudest right now.
